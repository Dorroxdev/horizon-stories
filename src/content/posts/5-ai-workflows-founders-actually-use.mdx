Most AI-for-founders content is theater. Someone tries a prompt, gets a clever output, posts a screenshot. Three weeks later they've stopped using it.

The workflows below are different. These are the five I actually use weekly. They've survived more than a quarter of real use. If I had to give up four of them, I'd be slower but still functional. If I had to give up all five, I'd need to hire someone within a month.

This is the boring middle. No hot takes, no "agents will replace founders", just the specific workflow patterns that compound.

## 1. Codebase-aware refactors via [primary coding tool]

The single highest-ROI AI workflow for me is using [Claude Code / Cursor / your-tool-of-choice] for cross-file refactors. Not single-file edits. Cross-file.

The specific pattern: I describe the refactor as a goal ("rename `getResourceBySlug` to `findResourceBySlug` and update all call sites", or "extract the auth middleware into a shared package and have both API routes import it"). The tool reads the relevant files, proposes a unified diff, I review, and ship.

What this replaces: 30-60 minutes of grep, manual edits, broken-test debugging.
What it costs: about 5 minutes of prompt-writing and review.
ROI: roughly 10x on time, and more on cognitive load.

The thing most "AI for coding" guides miss: the value isn't in the AI generating new code. It's in the AI doing the boring deterministic work (finding all call sites, updating imports, preserving formatting) faster than I can. The cleverness budget should be spent on review, not generation.

<Callout type="info">
The fastest AI workflows are usually the ones where the AI is doing search-and-substitute work at high accuracy, not the ones where the AI is being creative. Save the creativity for the human.
</Callout>

## 2. First-draft writing with a strict frame

I draft most of my long-form writing (including this post) with an LLM as a first-pass partner. The workflow is:

1. I write the outline myself. Five to seven bullets, including the conclusion. The outline is the post's spine; I do this without AI.
2. I prompt the model to expand each bullet into a paragraph, with explicit voice/tone constraints ("period form, no em dashes, specific examples not generic claims, first-person").
3. The output is a rough first draft. I then rewrite it line by line. Most of it gets replaced.

The model's first draft is rarely usable as-is. But it's faster for me to react to an existing draft than to stare at a blank page. The cognitive cost of "type the next paragraph from scratch" is higher than "fix this paragraph the model produced".

What this replaces: blank-page paralysis.
What it costs: model output that's [X]% rewritten by the time I publish.
ROI: I publish roughly [N]x more often than I did before this workflow.

**The trap to avoid:** trusting the first draft. If you don't rewrite, the post sounds like an LLM, and readers can tell. The model is your assistant, not your voice.

## 3. Tactical research with citation-required prompts

For any decision that needs external research ("should I use Stripe or Paddle for this", "what's the current best-practice for X"), I prompt with a citation-required instruction.

The pattern: "Research [QUESTION]. For every claim, include the source URL. Do not include claims without sources."

This filters out the model's hallucinations. If it can't cite a source, it usually invents one (which I can verify and discard), or it omits the claim (which is the desired outcome). Either way, the output is roughly [N]x more reliable than a free-form research prompt.

What this replaces: 30-60 minutes of manual Google + skim.
What it costs: 5-10 minutes of prompt-and-verify.
ROI: faster decisions on tooling choices, regulatory questions, market sizing. About 4x.

**The trap to avoid:** trusting the citations without clicking through. Models will cite real-looking URLs that 404 or that say something different than the claim. Verify the top [N] citations before acting on them.

## 4. Decision documentation in the moment

When I make a non-trivial decision (pick a library, change an architecture, kill a feature), I write a short decision record. The format is:

- **Context:** what we know
- **Decision:** what we did
- **Alternatives:** what we considered
- **Consequences:** what to watch for

I draft the decision record by talking it through with an LLM. Five minutes of voice-to-text "here's what I'm thinking" turns into a structured DEC-XXX entry that future-me can read.

This is a workflow I almost didn't include because it sounds boring. But it has compounded more than any other AI use I have. Six months of decision records have prevented me from re-litigating the same questions roughly [N] times.

What this replaces: re-debating settled decisions every few months.
What it costs: 5 minutes per decision.
ROI: continuous. Higher every quarter.

## 5. Pre-flight checks before any external email or post

Before sending any external email longer than three sentences, or publishing any public post, I run a pre-flight prompt:

"Read this draft. Flag anything that's: unclear, vague, off-brand, in a different voice than [previous example], factually unsupported, or longer than it needs to be."

The model is reliably good at this. It's catching the kind of mistakes I make when I'm tired, and it's faster than asking a human reviewer.

What this replaces: the third proofread.
What it costs: 30 seconds.
ROI: catches about [N]% of mistakes that would have made it through unaided. Has saved me [N] embarrassing public moments this year.

**The trap to avoid:** acting on every flag. The model will sometimes flag things that are intentional choices ("this is too direct"). Skim, accept the ones that land, ignore the rest.

## What I do NOT use AI for

Worth saying explicitly: there are categories where AI has been net-negative for me, and where I've stopped trying. The list is shorter than the list above but real:

- **Strategic decisions.** The model can argue any side of a strategic question convincingly. That makes it actively unhelpful when I'm trying to think clearly about something high-stakes.
- **Cold outreach personalization.** It produces uncanny-valley emails that recipients spot immediately. I write outreach by hand.
- **Editing emotional content.** The model tends to soften and generalize. If I'm writing something where the specificity is the point, the model is the wrong collaborator.
- **Initial customer interviews.** No model substitutes for the surprise of a real human telling you something you weren't expecting.

## The framework, distilled

The five workflows above share a pattern: the AI is doing high-accuracy, low-creativity work, and the human is doing the judgment work. When I flip that ratio (asking the model to be creative, treating the output as final), the ROI collapses.

If you're starting fresh and want to pick one to try first: workflow 1 (cross-file refactors in your codebase) is probably the highest-leverage. It pays back in the first session.

If you're already deep on AI workflows: workflow 4 (decision records) is the most underrated. Most founders don't write decision records at all. The ones who do, never want to go back.

The compounding here is real but quiet. None of these workflows feel like a breakthrough on the day you adopt them. Six months later, the absence of them is conspicuous.
