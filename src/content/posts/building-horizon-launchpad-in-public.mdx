A founder I respect once told me the most useful thing he'd ever read was a competitor's failed launch postmortem. Not because he wanted to gloat. Because everyone else had been telling him "ship faster" and "talk to users" while glossing over what those actually look like at 2am on a Tuesday with the build broken and the launch in 12 hours.

That stuck. Most founder stories you read are the sanitized retrospective. The opening paragraph is a milestone (raised X, hit Y MRR, sold to Z). The middle paragraphs are the polished narrative arc. The closing paragraph is the lesson, neatly extracted, with a CTA.

The real insight is in the messy middle. The pivot that wasn't a pivot, just a slow drift. The feature that took three rewrites and still isn't right. The week you ignored the dashboard because the dashboard was telling you something you didn't want to hear.

Horizon Launchpad is my attempt to ship the messy middle. To write about what I'm actually doing as I do it, including the parts that will be embarrassing in six months.

## Why now

I've been building products for [X years]. Most of that has been quiet. The work was good but I rarely wrote about it, partly because the people I was building for were already in the room, and partly because the broader internet looked, to me, like a poor signal-to-noise environment.

That's changed. Or my opinion of it has. Two specific things:

**1. The bar for "in public" has shifted from anecdote to receipts.** Five years ago, "building in public" was 280 characters and a vibe. Today the readers expect numbers, screenshots, repo links, and a real explanation of what didn't work. Higher bar, but also higher signal. The audience filters for substance.

**2. Most founder content is still being generated by people one step removed from the work.** Investors writing about founders. Operators writing about ten years ago. Coaches writing about clients. The actual operator-in-the-middle perspective is rare, and when it does appear, it's usually drowned in pitch deck cadence.

I'd rather be one of the people writing from the middle than reading from the outside.

## What "in public" means here, specifically

A lot of "building in public" content is theatrical. The numbers are real but the framing is staged: a year-end recap, a quarterly milestone post, a Twitter thread timed to a launch. That's fine, but it's not what I'm signing up for here.

For Horizon Launchpad specifically, "in public" means three things:

**Revenue transparency, but useful.** I'll publish the topline numbers and the breakdown when there's something to learn from them. What I won't do is hit-publish on a chart that goes up-and-to-the-right with no context, because that's vanity, not signal. The relevant question isn't "what did you make this month" but "what experiment generated that, and was the experiment worth repeating".

**Mistake transparency, including the small ones.** Big failure post-mortems are easy to write because they have a clear narrative arc. The harder ones are the small mistakes: the feature you shipped that didn't move the needle, the day you spent on the wrong thing, the integration you over-engineered. I'll write about those too. They compound more than the big failures, and most founder content ignores them entirely.

**Tactic transparency, with the receipts.** If I tell you a launch worked, I'll show you the email I sent, the open rate, the CTA, the conversion. If a tactic flopped, I'll show what I expected vs what I got. The goal is for a future founder reading this to be able to clone the tactic, not just admire it.

## What I'm planning to write about

The pillars on this site are five: founder stories, expert playbooks, AI strategy, failure post-mortems, guides and frameworks. They're not arbitrary. Each one corresponds to a kind of post that I either wanted to read and couldn't find, or wrote for myself and decided to publish.

- **Founder stories** are first-person operator narratives, including this one. No third-party retellings. If I didn't live it, I'm not telling it.
- **Expert playbooks** are how-to deep dives with actual code or workflows you can copy. The newsletter-first launch post that's queued up next is one of these.
- **AI strategy** is the boring middle of building with AI: which workflows actually save time, which are theater, which break in three months.
- **Failure post-mortems** are the parts I'd usually leave out. The first one in the queue is a six-month retrospective on what I'd build differently.
- **Guides and frameworks** are the reusable artifacts. Templates, checklists, decision trees. Same shape as the resources I publish, but unbundled.

I'll prioritize quality over cadence. One real post a week is the target. Two would be nice. Zero is acceptable if nothing has changed and I'd be repeating myself.
